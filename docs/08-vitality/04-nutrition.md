## Water

- Drink plain water, in the morning and before sleep, to avoid dehydration at night. About 1.5 litres of drinks a day is a floor, not a target.
- Thirst is a late signal. Keep water within arm's reach, so drinking costs neither a trip nor a decision. A bottle you must go and fill is a bottle you don't drink.
- Mineral and medicinal waters are a treatment, not a substitute. Taken as the daily drink they are their own slow problem.

## Food

- Eat real food, mostly plants. Ultra-processed food is engineered for overconsumption, not nourishment: calorie-dense, nutrient-poor, tuned to slip past your satiety signal so you keep eating. Chronic reliance tracks weight gain and metabolic dysfunction. Processed meat and fast food also carry most of the day's salt. A "protein" bar is often mostly sugar — read the label.
- Eat a wide range, not a fixed menu. The gut flora grows more diverse on many different plants a week; buy one food you have never tried on each shopping trip. Rotate protein sources; keep both animal and plant fats — they carry vitamins A, D, E and K. A diet narrows quietly over the years, so review it twice a year.
- Know more than five vegetables. Cucumber and lettuce carry almost no fiber. Most adults eat less than the 25–30 g a day that guidelines advise. Raise fiber step by step — a sudden jump brings bloating.
- Eat enough. Busy people under-eat without noticing, and the fatigue looks like illness. Training on too little food is paid for in muscle: the body breaks it down for protein.
- A fully plant-based diet cannot supply vitamin B12. It needs a B12 supplement and a plan for protein, iron and calcium.
- Plant drinks are not milk. Most carry far less protein, and calcium only when fortified. Milk is a good food unless a tested allergy or intolerance says otherwise — see [Warning Signs](02-vitality.md#warning-signs).
- Change the diet in small steps and keep the change. Sudden, strict diets mostly end in regain.
- Eat protein first — it keeps blood sugar stable. Prioritize lean protein: meat, eggs, cottage cheese. Less bread.
- Protein alone under-fuels a long working block. When the morning demands sustained work, pair it with a slow carbohydrate — buckwheat, rice, oats. Fast sugar plus processed meat is the worst of both: the energy does not last and the protein never arrives.
- Cook in batches, within the days refrigerated food keeps. It makes the good option the ready one, which is most of why the poor option wins. Protein needs cooking while fast carbohydrate is on every corner, so a protein shortfall is usually a planning failure.
- No smoking. Limit alcohol, sugar, fried food, and late-night eating. Alcohol loosens the gut wall's barrier: an occasional drink heals in between, a steady habit does not. It helps neither digestion nor a cold.
- Get vitamins from food. A blood test shows today's level, not your stores or your intake, and excess doses do their own harm. Any supplement is a question for a clinician, not a default.
- Take antibiotics only as prescribed, and finish the course. Prescription weight-loss medication is a clinical decision.
- Acid blockers (proton pump inhibitors, such as omeprazole) are not a shield to take before a feast. Stomach acid is part of digestion, and long use without a reason is linked to low B12 and magnesium and to gut infections. Digestive enzymes, too, only when a clinician finds a reason.

## Timing

- A long gap between dinner and breakfast suits some people. Autophagy — the cell's own recycling, whose mechanisms earned Yoshinori Ohsumi the 2016 Nobel Prize in yeast — is often cited for it, though the human fasting window that engages it is not settled.
- Within a meal, control blood sugar by order: vegetables first, then protein and fat, then carbs. Walk after eating.
- The first meal sets the day's blood-sugar tone. The brain needs fuel, not a sugar hit.
- A sweet breakfast — sugary porridge, toast with jam, juice, even "healthy" honey — spikes blood sugar. Build a low-glycemic first meal instead: eggs, a handful of nuts or a spoon of olive oil, and vegetables or greens.
- Coffee is not a strain to avoid. Up to three or four cups a day is linked to lower risk of liver disease and type 2 diabetes in large observational studies; reflux is a reason to cut it. Delay the first cup rather than taking it on waking, and finish before the afternoon so it does not cost that night's sleep.
- Eat on a schedule: three meals, no long daytime gap. Five or six small meals are a pattern for acute conditions such as an open ulcer, not a rule for healthy people. A skipped lunch leaves bile standing in the gallbladder. Regularity comes before any diet — restrictions laid on chaotic meals fail. Focus mutes hunger, and skipped meals are not cancelled: they return as an evening binge, which kills the next morning's appetite and restarts the loop. Spread food across the day in similar portions; when work hides hunger, set a reminder.
- Keep dinner light — which only works if breakfast and lunch happened.

## Gut Flora

| Kind | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Probiotic** | Live bacteria taken as a product |
| **Prebiotic** | Fiber and other food that your own flora feeds on |
| **Synbiotic** | A probiotic and a prebiotic in one product |
| **Postbiotic** | Inactivated bacteria or their products, with no live cells |

- The gut flora is a balance, not a war. Opportunistic species are normal residents and do harm only when they overgrow; true pathogens, such as *Salmonella*, should be absent. More "good" bacteria is not always better.
- Food, stress, sleep and alcohol shape the balance. The flora takes part in digestion and in making neurotransmitters, and gut and brain signal both ways through the vagus nerve: anxiety upsets the gut, and an upset gut weighs on mood — see [Recovery → Stress](03-recovery.md#stress). Treating depression through the flora is still research, not practice.
- Probiotics are not a reset. Each person's flora is as individual as a fingerprint, and most taken strains pass through without settling. The evidence is for specific strains in specific cases, such as diarrhea after antibiotics; taken at random for months, they can bring bloating instead.
- Feed the flora you have: varied plants and enough fiber — see [Food](#food).

*NOTE*: this page describes habits, not treatment. Medication, supplements and any restrictive pattern are clinical decisions.
