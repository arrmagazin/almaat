## Sleep

The habit the rest depend on. Quality depends on light, temperature, food, caffeine, screens, and regularity.

- Sleep is active cleaning, not rest. During sleep the glymphatic system clears metabolic waste — including the adenosine whose build-up is felt as sleep pressure — and the day is consolidated into `Memory`. Caffeine masks the adenosine signal without clearing it: it borrows alertness rather than creating it, and coffee that has stopped working is a measure of the debt.
- Sleep as much as you need, and go to bed at the same time every night.
- Regularity beats early rising. Melatonin release is driven by darkness rather than by the clock, so a consistent schedule and a dark room matter more than a particular bedtime. Regularity predicts long-term health more strongly than duration does, and a stable late schedule beats an unstable early one.
- Anchor the wake end. Falling asleep cannot be commanded; rising can — and a fixed wake time is what makes evening sleepiness arrive on schedule. The internal clock is a gene loop: the PER protein builds up overnight, shuts down its own gene and decays, about once a day.[^5] It runs slightly longer than a day, so an unanchored schedule drifts later; sleeping in on a day off restarts the drift. Rise on the first alarm — snoozed five-minute stretches restore nothing.
- Sleep debt is not fully repayable. Sleep runs in cycles across the night, and a short night is not made good by a long weekend. Recovery is slow and uneven: reaction speed returns in a day, planning and self-control in two or three — so the day after a short night belongs to routine, not decisions or heavy training. Protecting sleep in advance is cheaper than repaying it.
- Teenagers need 8–10 hours, and puberty shifts their clock later. Sleep medicine advises school starts at 8:30 or later.[^1]
- Do not drive after a sleepless night. 17–19 hours awake impairs performance as much as a blood alcohol level of 0.05%, and 24 hours as much as 0.10%.[^2]
- Deprivation hides itself. Weeks of six-hour nights bring performance down to the level of a night without sleep, while felt sleepiness levels off early — the faculty that judges the deficit is the first one it degrades.
- Chronically short sleep is associated with lower immunity and worse cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes. It also tilts the hormonal axis — cortisol up, growth hormone and testosterone down — so on the same diet the loss shifts from fat to muscle.
- Keep the room dark, cool, quiet, and aired. A room too warm interferes with the deep phases, and in a closed bedroom carbon dioxide climbs through the night.
- Light reaching the eyes suppresses melatonin, so darkness matters as much as time in bed.
- Caffeine taken even six hours before bed cuts around an hour of sleep — measured objectively, unnoticed subjectively.
- Finish the last full meal three to four hours before bed. Falling asleep needs the core temperature to drop, and digestion holds it up; a light snack is harmless.
- Alcohol doesn't help you sleep. It fragments sleep architecture, so time in bed stops corresponding to rest.
- Poor sleep spreads. A partner who sleeps badly tends to cost you sleep too.
- Keep a one-hour wind-down and read a book — the body settles into a schedule it can predict. A warm bath before bed helps, as does avoiding bright light in the hours before it. A breathing pattern with a longer exhale than inhale helps at the point of falling asleep. Stimulation right before bed follows you in: after a game, a feed, or an argument, the first hours of sleep stay shallow even when falling asleep succeeds.
- Set an evening alarm. The body signals waking but never bedtime, and evening self-control is the day's weakest — hand the decision to stop to an external signal instead of a tired brain.

## Sunlight

- Get outdoor light early in the day. It sets your body clock.
- Daylight soon after waking anchors the circadian clock, which is what sets the evening melatonin rise. Get it without sunglasses.
- Longer on cloudy days than bright ones. Avoid midday glare and protect your skin in the midday sun.
- Sunburn, not steady exposure, drives melanoma. A history of sunburn about doubles the risk, intermittent exposure raises it, and steady daily exposure does not.[^3]
- Sunset light signals the body to prepare for sleep.

## Stress

- Chronic stress degrades immune function. Acute stress builds it — exercise is good stress.
- Expose yourself to manageable challenges to build resilience. Small, steady action beats bursts of intensity. Stress inoculation — rehearsing a stressor in safe, graded doses — lowers anxiety and protects performance under pressure, more so the longer it is trained.[^4]
- Rest and recovery are mandatory, not optional. Stillness restores focus: a daily period of doing nothing.
- Move your body, connect with people, breathe deeply to release stress.
- Physiological sigh: a full inhale, a second short inhale, then a long exhale.
- Ground yourself. Unhurried time outdoors lowers the arousal that indoor days sustain.

What the response costs you is in [Energy](05-energy.md); under a real threat the same system runs at full force — see [the body under threat](../07-sustain/06-security.md#the-body-under-threat).

## Breathing

- Breathe through your nose. Use your diaphragm.
- Slow nasal breathing lowers arousal, and is the fastest lever available on it.
- Never practice intensive breathing while driving or in water.

*NOTE*: this page describes habits, not treatment. Persistent sleep problems are a clinical matter.
